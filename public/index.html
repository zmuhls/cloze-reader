About this Project

In 1953, Wilson Taylor deleted words from a passage at regular intervals, asked readers to supply them from context, and called the result a cloze test. Decades later, BERT's developers masked tokens in the same way and rewarded a neural network for predicting them from surrounding context. Taylor wanted to measure reading comprehension; Devlin wanted to induce vector representations. Harris and Firth had already supplied the shared premise, that words derive meaning from the company they keep, and both procedures formalized it. Cloze Reader returns the fill-in-the-blank task to human readers at a moment when machines perform it at scale.

Each round draws a passage from Project Gutenberg and removes one or more words, selected by an AI model rather than at fixed intervals. Read the surrounding context and infer the missing word. A chat panel offers hints about part of speech, sentence role, and synonymy without giving the answer directly. Advancing through levels adds blanks per passage and shifts the vocabulary from common to challenging. Scores and streaks track progress across rounds, and a leaderboard records the highest levels reached. Word selection runs with non-zero temperature decoding, so the same passage can yield different blanks on different runs.

Passages stream from the Hugging Face Datasets API into a Cloudflare Workers backend built with Hono. Word selection, hint generation, and passage contextualization each run as separate calls to Google's Gemma-3-27B via OpenRouter, isolated so that selection cannot drift toward words the hint system handles fluently. Gutenberg texts are the source archive. They are canonical pretraining data, present in The Pile and its successors, already absorbed into parameter weights and disaggregated from their narrative forms. Cloze Reader restores those flattened texts to the surface and asks human readers to engage them at a pace the model's low-latency engine cannot match. Source code lives at github.com/zmuhls/cloze-reader.
